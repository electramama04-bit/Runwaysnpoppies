## Body

```
There was blue painter's tape on the classroom floor, in a square, around my
son's desk.

It was there so he would stay inside it.

He was 6.

I want to be fair about this. The teacher was not cruel. She had too many kids and
not enough hands, and she needed him to stop getting up. The tape was a tool. It
worked, the way a fence works.

But I stood in that classroom and looked at the square, and I thought about what
it teaches a six year old to be marked off from everybody else. Not what it
teaches him about sitting still. What it teaches him about himself.

I pulled him out that year.

I did not have a plan. I had a job at Boeing, a husband, another son, and no idea
what I was doing. What I had was one piece of information, which is that my son
will sit perfectly still, for an hour, for an airplane.

So I built the whole thing around airplanes.

History is the Wright Brothers, and the pilots who flew in the war, and my dad in
an Air Force uniform, and the postwar boom that built the company I work for.

Math is flight physics. Altitude. Drag. How much fuel to get from here to there,
and what happens if you are wrong.

Science is weather and aerodynamics, and why a nuclear cooling tower is shaped
the way it is, which we learned standing at the bottom of one at Satsop.

Reading is pilot memoirs and the letters veterans wrote home.

He is 9 now, in fifth grade, and he does not need tape.

I am a Boeing tool maker. Sixteen years. I build jigs and fixtures for the 777,
which means I make the tools that hold a part in the exact same place every
single time, so every part that comes off the line is identical. If the jig
moves, you can never tell whose fault a bad part was.

That is also how I teach him to run an experiment. Same ramp. Same starting line.
Same person on the stopwatch. Then change one thing only. Scientists call it a
controlled variable. I call it a jig. Same idea.

I write our school year at the kitchen table before my shift, in the dark, with
coffee.

I am also the Chaplain of VFW Auxiliary Post 969 here in Tacoma. My dad served in
the Air Force in a foreign war and he is the Quartermaster at our Post. I still
cry every single time I hear the Star Spangled Banner. I used to be embarrassed
about that. I am not anymore.

Runways for my son. Poppies for my dad.

That is the name, and now you know why.

Here is what this newsletter is. One letter a week. What we built, what worked,
what did not work, and the printable that came out of it. I am not going to tell
you I have it figured out. I am going to show you what we actually did, including
the weeks it falls apart, because those weeks are most of them.

Three things are already on the site and they are free right now. No email, no
account, nothing.

The Four Forces. Three pages on lift, weight, thrust and drag, taught through a
kite, a boat, and a monster truck, because all three are fighting the same thing.

The Flight Test Report. Two pages. It is the report real test engineers write
after a program, and it doubles as a writing sample and a science assessment for
the annual review.

The Hours Log. A year long tracker for a Washington homeschool that adds up your
hours by subject on its own, so you are not doing it in a panic in June.

Take them. runwaysnpoppies.com

If your kid does not fit in the square, he was never supposed to.

Shirley
```

## Why this one

It is the origin story, told long. The Reel gives it 30 seconds and the About
page gives it 249 words. Neither has room for the teacher who was not cruel, or
the jig rule, or the part where she had no plan. Those are what make it true
instead of tidy.

It also does the two jobs a first letter has to do. It explains why this exists,
and it hands over three real things on the way out.

## What NOT to make the first post

- A welcome note about what is coming. Nobody subscribes to a promise.
- The full Flight and Friction unit. That is the first gated file, and gating
  before there are subscribers gates an empty room.
