# CAPTION A — Ocean Shores day
**Sun, Sep 13 · ~3 min · Warm, joyful, low stakes**

> Our classroom on Saturday was a beach, and the lesson plan was "find out what the
> ocean does to things."

Here's what Ocean Shores taught us in about six hours, and I promise you not one minute
of it was on a worksheet.

**The kite taught wind.** Not "wind is moving air" — actual wind, the kind that yanks your
arms straight and then drops you without warning. We flew it for maybe forty minutes and
he figured out on his own that you don't fight the pull, you angle into it. I build 777
fixtures for a living and I want to tell you: that's lift. He learned lift with his shoulders.

**The RC boat taught defeat.** Four seconds. The Pacific Ocean took it in four seconds, and
he laughed so hard he sat down in the sand. There is no textbook page that teaches a kid
that the ocean is stronger than his stuff. He had to hand it over and watch.

**The truck taught patience,** mostly mine.

**And the pizza taught physics,** but that one's a whole post of its own next week.

Somewhere in there he asked me why the waves come in sets instead of one at a time, and I
didn't know, so we looked it up in the truck on the way home and now we both know.

That's the part I want other moms to hear. **You don't have to know the answer.** You have
to be willing to stop and go find it. That's the entire method.

The world is our classroom, and Saturday it was 45 degrees and soaking wet and the best
school day we've had all year.

# CAPTION B — BLUE TAPE (the anchor — PIN THIS TO YOUR PAGE)
**Wed, Sep 16 · 4–5 min read**
**Post this the same hour the Reel goes up. Noah reads it first. He gets a veto.**
**⚠️ AGE CHECK: this caption says "nine" three times and "fourth-grade" twice. If the tape
happened last school year he was eight. Fix every instance before you post — that number
is what people will quote, and it's what they'll check.**

> They put blue tape around my son. So I built him a runway instead.

His fourth-grade teacher put blue painter's tape on the floor around his desk before he
walked in the door on the first day of school.

Not after an incident. Before. Before she had met him. Before he had said one word in her
classroom. The box was already on the floor waiting for him when he came in with his new
backpack.

The rule was that his chair, his feet, his hands, and his body stayed inside the tape.

He was nine.

I asked what he had done to earn it and I was told, in a kind voice, that he had a lot of
energy and this would help him "stay regulated." I want to be fair here — I don't think
that woman got up in the morning wanting to hurt my child. I think she was handed thirty
kids and no help and she reached for the tool she had.

But I need you to understand what a nine-year-old boy learns standing in a taped box on
the first day of school. He does not learn to be still. He learns *there is something wrong
with me and everybody can see it.* He learns it in front of his friends. He learns it before
lunch.

He came home and asked me if he was bad.

---

I'm a toolmaker at Boeing. I build the jigs and fixtures that hold a 777 together while
it's being built — the structures that hold the part in exactly the right position so it
comes out true.

So let me tell you something about fixtures.

**When a fixture is built wrong, the part comes out wrong. Every single time. And it is
never the part's fault.**

You can inspect that part all day. You can write it up, tag it, put it in a box, tape a
line around it. It will still be wrong, because the problem was never in the part. The
problem was in the thing built to hold it.

They kept inspecting my son. Nobody looked at the fixture.

---

So I pulled him out.

We started homeschooling in April. Since then his classroom has been a beach in Ocean
Shores, an abandoned nuclear cooling tower in Grays Harbor County that most people in this
state have never heard of, a kite string, a kitchen counter covered in pizza dough, and a
fence at an airfield with a 737 rolling past twenty feet away.

He is not behind. He is ahead, and more than that, **he is himself again.** He asks
questions in the truck now. He didn't do that for most of last year.

I'm a Christian, and I'll say the part that matters most to me plainly: God did not make
a defective boy and send him to me for correction. He made a boy with a full tank and put
him in a room with no runway. My job was never to shrink him down to fit the room. It was
to go find him a bigger room.

**They built him a box. I built him a runway.**

---

I'm starting to post what we do, because I know I am not the only mother who has sat in
that meeting. If you are in it right now — if there's tape on the floor around your kid,
literal or not — I want you to know two things.

Your kid is not broken.

And you are allowed to leave.

I'll be sharing where we go, what we learn, what it costs, and what I get wrong, because I
get plenty wrong. Follow along if it helps. Bring your own kid.

**The world is our classroom — especially the parts with wings, waves, and weird landmarks.**

# CAPTION C — Pizza dough physics
**Sun, Sep 20 · ~2 min · Deliberate cool-down after Blue Tape**

> The pizza dough started flying and the science lesson started itself.

We were making dinner. He was throwing the dough because throwing the dough is the best
part, and it kept coming out lopsided, and he got frustrated, and then he figured it out
before I could say anything.

**Spin it flat and it stays round. Spin it crooked and it folds.**

That's angular momentum. That's the same reason a wheel stays up when it's rolling and
falls over when it stops. That's the same reason a propeller works. He does not know those
words yet and it does not matter even slightly, because he knows the *thing*, and the words
will show up later and stick to something real.

This is what I mean when I say the world is our classroom. I did not plan this. It cost
zero dollars and one bag of flour on the floor.

If you want one homeschool tip from me this week, here it is: **when your kid figures
something out on his own, do not explain it back to him.** Just say "huh — show me again."

#MakeLifeFun

# CAPTION D — Kids used to be able to play like boys
**Wed, Sep 23 · 4–5 min read**
**Calm, not angry. Read it out loud before you post it. If any sentence sounds like a**
**fight, cut it.**

> They brought a terrorism screener into a meeting about my nine-year-old, because he
> pointed his fingers at his friend and said "pew."

That's the whole incident. He was on the playground. He made a finger-gun. His friend made
one back. They were playing.

I want to say the fair thing first, before I say anything else, because I mean it:
**schools are terrified for a reason.** I'm not going to stand here in 2026 and pretend
that fear came from nowhere. Every teacher in this country has run a drill. Every one of
them has done the math on their own classroom door. That is real and I am not making light
of it.

But here is what I keep running into, and I'd genuinely like somebody to explain it to me.

---

I grew up in Ohio. We learned to shoot about a day after we learned to walk. There were
gun racks in the back windows of trucks in the high school parking lot and not one adult
blinked, because everybody understood what they were looking at.

That world was not perfect. I'm not selling you a postcard of it. Plenty was wrong with it.

But we still knew the difference between **a boy playing** and **a boy in trouble.**

That was a skill the adults had. You watched a kid, you knew him, you knew his house, and
you could tell. It took judgment, and judgment takes knowing somebody.

Somewhere we traded judgment for a checklist. And a checklist can't tell the difference,
so it treats every kid like the second one. My son didn't get evaluated by a person who
knew him. He got run through a process.

He came out of that meeting knowing that adults thought he might be dangerous. He was nine.

---

Here's where his hands were this month.

They folded a paper airplane and re-folded it four times to fix the trim. They wrote in a
nature journal on a beach in Ocean Shores. They drove a remote control boat straight into
the Pacific Ocean until the Pacific Ocean won in four seconds. They touched the inside wall
of a nuclear cooling tower that was built the year before I was in high school and never
used a single day.

**Same hands.** Nobody had to take anything away from him. Somebody just finally gave him
something to do with them.

---

I'm not asking any school to stop caring about safety. I'm asking a smaller question, and
it's an honest one:

**When did play become a threat — and who decided that a checklist knows a child better
than the people who love him?**

If you had this same meeting, I'd like to hear about it. I think there are more of us than
anybody's counted.

# CAPTION E — Satsop
**Sun, Sep 27 · ~3 min · Cool-down + curiosity payoff**

> There are two 481-foot nuclear cooling towers about 90 minutes from Tacoma that have
> never cooled anything, and most Washington families have never heard of them.

Satsop, Grays Harbor County. Construction started in the 1970s. The money ran out, the
project died, and the towers just... stayed. They are enormous and they are silent and you
can stand inside one and yell and wait a genuinely uncomfortable amount of time for it to
come back to you.

What my son learned there had nothing to do with nuclear power.

He learned that **adults start enormous things and don't always finish them.** He walked
around inside the biggest unfinished homework assignment in the state of Washington.

I build fixtures for 777s at Boeing. Everything I make exists to hold something in the
right place until it's strong enough to hold itself. Standing in that tower, I kept
thinking: somebody built this thinking it would matter, and it never got the chance.

That's a real thing for a nine-year-old to chew on, and he did chew on it, the whole ride
home.

**Go see it.** It's free, it's weird, it's ninety minutes out, and your kid will talk about
it for a week.

# CAPTION F — One month of #MakeLifeFun
**Sun, Oct 4 · ~3 min · Montage recap**

> One month ago I started posting about pulling my son out of school. Here's where his
> classroom has been since.

A beach. A nuclear cooling tower. A kitchen counter. An airfield fence. The inside of my
truck, three separate times, looking something up because neither of us knew.

I told you at the start that I'd share what we learn and what I get wrong. So here's what
I got wrong this month: **I overplanned.** Twice I had a whole day mapped out and he found

schedule. Both times I let it go and both times it was the best part of the day.

Here's what I got right: **I stopped explaining things back to him.**

And here's what surprised me — the messages. Dozens of you have written to tell me about
your own meeting, your own tape on the floor, your own kid who got labeled before anybody
knew him. I read every one. I'm sorry it's this many of us. I'm glad it's not just me.

Next month we're going deeper into aviation, because that's my lane and it's his favorite:
Museum of Flight, Paine Field, and I'm working on something I can't announce yet.

**So tell me — where should we take school next?** Weird landmarks, wild places, or wings.
I'll take the one with the most comments.
